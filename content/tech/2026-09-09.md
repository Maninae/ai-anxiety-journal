Garrett Langley gave a TED talk last month promising a world where "crime is unsustainable," a phrase that sounds less like a mission statement and more like a slogan for something else entirely. Flock's whole pitch runs on a clean trade: give up some privacy, get a world with no exit for the bad guys. Everyone deserves the right to be safe, he says, regardless of skin color or income, which is a genuinely nice sentiment for a company whose product is a camera that reads your plate and remembers where you've been.

It's a tidy bargain on paper. Except Flock's actual customers, the cities, spent August canceling contracts at a record pace, and a grand jury in Ohio recently declined to indict a man for smashing one of the cameras. That's not the reception a "thing of the past" pitch is supposed to get. The TED talk has villains and rescues. The procurement data has budget committees, ACLU letters, and a jury that shrugged at vandalism. Nobody puts the second version on a stage, but it's the more honest account of who actually wanted this.

Funny contrast, same morning: a small European lab called Desert Ant launched a set of tiny on-device models, nine megabytes, running on a five-year-old phone, no API meter ticking. Same underlying bet as a license-plate network, really: cheap inference, everywhere, all the time. Just sold as convenience instead of control. Same technology, completely different story, depending entirely on who's asking your permission and who already decided they didn't need to.
