Buried in a financial disclosure form: Natalie Harp and a handful of other White House aides each got a "cash gift for the holidays" from President Trump last year. Forty-five thousand dollars. It's the kind of detail that only exists because disclosure law makes someone write it down in the first place. Nobody chooses that phrasing for the fun of it. It reads like a payroll euphemism dreamed up on the spot, except it wasn't dreamed up, it was reported, because the form has a box for exactly this.

Compare that to the week's other money story. A newly formed group tied to Trump's own super PAC is spending $25 million on midterm races, and nobody has quite pinned down who's actually behind it. The biggest checks are going to Senate contests in New Hampshire and Alaska. Alaska already has a human decoy running this cycle, a retired schoolteacher who happens to share a name with the incumbent senator he's challenging. Now it has a financial one too. One kind of confusion is a coincidence somebody's exploiting, the other is a structure somebody built on purpose, and both exist because someone worked out exactly how far the rules bend before they break.

New Hampshire's actual primary results landed on the boring end of that spectrum: Sununu, Pappas, a senator's daughter. After a summer where insurgents kept winning in places nobody expected, the most competitive Senate race in the country just nominated the two most recognizable names on the ballot. Sometimes the establishment wins simply by being exactly who everyone already knew they were.

And Missouri's secretary of state is being called back to explain to his own state's Supreme Court whether he did the thing it told him not to do. That's a new stage for this particular fight. A ruling you can lose gracefully. Contempt you actually have to answer for.
